Which plants in this yard should go?

LawnGuard is a vision system for a future backyard robot. It looks at a plant and answers keep, remove, or not sure, based on what you want in your lawn. The goal is to treat only the weeds instead of spraying herbicide over the whole yard.

How it works

One pipeline, three ways to decide

Photos are resized and split so that pictures of the same plant never land in both training and testing. Then each photo goes through one of three components.

Experiment 1

Recognize the species

Small CNNs (ResNet-18, MobileNetV3-Small, EfficientNet-B0) are fine-tuned with transfer learning. A keep list turns the species into keep or remove.

Experiment 2

Group photos without labels

A frozen DINOv2 model turns each photo into 384 numbers. K-means groups similar photos so a person labels a few groups instead of every photo.

Experiment 3

Learn your preferences

From a handful of your keep and remove examples, a simple rule decides each new plant. When it is unsure, it says so and asks you.

Experiments

The three experiments in detail

Experiment 1

Which small network is accurate and fast enough for a robot?

  • 45 training runs across three CNNs, five settings and three seeds. Mean macro-F1 was 92.2%.
  • EfficientNet-B0 was the most accurate; ResNet-18 was the fastest on the GPU.
  • MobileNetV3-Small, built for phones, was slower than ResNet-18 on the GPU. The ranking may change on a Raspberry Pi.
  • Less training data hurt the most: 25% of the data cost 5.3 points.

Experiment 2

Can the computer sort weed photos with no labels at all?

  • DINOv2 + K-means matched the real species far better than color and texture features: ARI 0.47 versus 0.09, purity 67%.
  • HDBSCAN left 74% of photos unassigned, so it was not useful here.
  • Look-alike species such as Lantana and Siam weed were merged into one group.
  • The computer could not find the right number of species by itself. The silhouette score barely changed between 9 and 11 groups.
Experiment 3 · Backyard photos

How many examples does a homeowner need to give?

  • Five to ten examples per class were enough. Most of the gain came from the first five.
  • With ten examples, logistic regression removed 98–100% of broadleaf weeds and none of the turfgrass.
  • Grass-like weeds were the hard part. A crabgrass photo is mostly lawn, so the whole-photo embedding looks like grass.
  • The "not sure" answer cut wanted plants marked for removal from 17.3% to 4.0% for a user who keeps clover.

Beyond weeds

A design principle for AI that adapts to each user

The correct answer here depends on the person: one homeowner grows clover on purpose, another pulls it. Instead of retraining one model for everyone, LawnGuard keeps a shared model frozen and adds a small personal layer that learns from each user. The same loop could help commercial AI systems learn from user supervision on top of an existing model.

1. RepresentA frozen foundation model, shared by everyone, turns each input into an embedding.
2. OrganizeYour unlabeled photos are clustered, so you label groups instead of single photos.
3. PersonalizeA few-shot rule learns your keep/remove choices from a handful of examples.
4. DeferWhen unsure, it asks you. Your answer becomes a new example, closing the loop.

Why it is practical: nothing big is retrained, your personal data is about 138 kB and can stay on your device, and an example can be deleted to undo it. Where it stops: the personal layer cannot separate plants that the shared model sees as the same, which is why crabgrass is missed.

Limitations and next steps

What this pilot study does not show yet

Small backyard dataset

922 photos, with only two to six distinct plants for several types.

Next: more plants, several sessions and lighting conditions.

Grass-like weeds

Crabgrass and nutsedge hide inside the lawn, so whole-photo features miss them.

Next: look at image patches or segment each plant first.

Speed measured on a GPU

The robot will use a small computer, not a data-center GPU.

Next: benchmark all three CNNs on a Raspberry Pi.

A camera shortcut in public data

Public grass photos came from a different camera, which made grass too easy to spot.

Next: train and test on photos from the same camera.

Leon Du

Developer and lead researcher - Holmdel High School, NJ

Leon Du is a student at Holmdel High School in New Jersey who loves robotics and plans to study engineering. His interest in robots led to a practical question: how could a backyard robot tell weeds from the plants a family wants to keep, so that it treats only the weeds instead of spraying the whole lawn?

LawnGuard is his answer so far. The project compares small neural networks for speed and accuracy, sorts weed photos without labels, and learns each homeowner's keep/remove choices from a few examples, tested on real backyard photos. Next, he wants to run the system on a Raspberry Pi and connect it to a robot.
